Economic Thinking

The first lesson of economics is scarcity: there is never enough of anything to fully satisfy all those who want it. The first lesson of politics is to disregard the first lesson of economics. Thomas Sowell

After this chapter you will be able to

  • explain scarcity, opportunity cost and incentives with examples from life and from the market;
  • show why a price ceiling creates a shortage and a price floor creates a surplus;
  • explain why two countries gain from trade even when one is better at everything;
  • connect these ideas to the exchange rate: the exchange rate is a price too.

Fundamentals · фундаментален анализAnalysis of the causes of a move: economy, rates, politics, capital flows.

●16.1 Scarcity: there are no solutions, only trade-offs

Scarce does not mean "rare". Scarce means that the sum of what everyone wants is more than what there is. Your time is scarce. A country's money is scarce. Market liquidity at 03:00 is scarce.

Once something is scarce, you have to choose. And every choice is a trade-off: you get one thing by giving up another. That is why an economist rarely talks about "solutions" and instead about what you gain and lose with each option.

StudentSo why do politicians always have solutions and economists only trade-offs?

MentorBecause "solutions" win elections and "trade-offs" win arguments. Only one of the two gets voted on.

Mentor's note

A central bank lives exactly in this world. If it raises rates, it brings inflation down but slows the economy and employment. If it cuts, it helps growth but risks inflation. There is no free move. That is why every rate decision creates winners and losers, and that moves the currency (Chapter 18).

Scarcity · оскъдностWants exceed the available resources, so choices have to be made.
Trade-off · компромисGetting something by giving up something else.

●16.2 Opportunity cost

The real cost of something is not only what you pay. It is the value of the best alternative you give up. If you spend an hour watching a series, its cost is the hour you could have spent studying, sleeping or working.

In markets this is everywhere. Cash in your account "costs nothing" until you see a government bond paying 4% a year. Then every year in cash costs you 4%. Gold pays no interest; when rates rise, the opportunity cost of holding it rises and gold often gets cheaper (Chapter 22).

Watch on Forex Factory

When you see a rate decision (for example the "Federal Funds Rate"), think opportunity cost. A higher US rate makes holding dollars more attractive than other currencies and than gold. That is the first reason currencies react to rates.

Opportunity cost · алтернативна ценаThe value of the best choice you passed up.
Yield · доходностWhat percentage per year an asset pays, for example a bond.

●16.3 Incentives: people respond

People do more of what they are rewarded for and less of what they pay for. It sounds obvious, but entire laws are written as if it were not true. Sowell repeats one rule: judge a policy by the incentives it creates, not by the goals it announces.

Prices are the strongest incentive. Profit says "do more". Loss says "stop, the resources are needed elsewhere". That is why a market economy is a system of profits and losses. Without the losses the signal is only half there.

Myth

Reality

A seller can ask any price. It becomes real only if someone pays it. Competition limits prices, not kindness. When house prices fall, it does not mean sellers have become better people.

Incentive · стимулA reward or penalty that changes behaviour.
Moral hazard · морален рискTaking more risk when someone else will pay for the loss.

●●16.4 Prices as messages, and what happens when you ban them

A price is a message. If coffee gets more expensive, you do not need to know there was frost in Brazil. Seeing the price is enough for you to drink a little less, and for a farmer somewhere else to plant more. Millions of people coordinate without anyone giving them orders.

When the state forbids a price from moving, the scarcity does not disappear. Only the message does. There are two kinds of price control:

  • A ceiling below the market price, for example rent control. Result: a shortage, queues, a black market, worse quality.
  • A floor above the market price, for example minimum purchase prices for grain. Result: a surplus that someone has to buy up, store or destroy.
Mentor's note

A fixed exchange rate is a price control on the currency. When a country keeps its currency artificially strong, dollars are "unavailable" except on the black market. Argentina, Venezuela, Nigeria and Egypt have all been through this. In the end the rate is let go and corrects sharply. Chapter 27 explains exactly how a fixed rate breaks.

Price ceiling · таванA legal maximum price. If it is below the market price, it creates a shortage.
Price floor · подA legal minimum price. If it is above the market price, it creates a surplus.
Shortage · недостигThe quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied at a given price.

●●16.5 Comparative advantage: why countries trade

Trade is not a zero-sum game. Both sides gain, otherwise they would not trade. The most surprising idea in economics is that this holds even when one country is better at everything.

Sowell's example: in the US one worker makes 500 chairs or 200 TVs a month. In Canada one worker makes 450 chairs or 100 TVs. The US is better at both. But in Canada one TV "costs" 4.5 chairs, while in the US only 2.5. Canada gives up less when it makes chairs. That is comparative advantage.

What does this have to do with currencies? Comparative advantage decides what a country exports. Australia exports iron ore, Canada and Norway oil, New Zealand dairy. That is why the prices of those goods move their currencies (Chapter 20). When trade is closed off with tariffs, these flows change and the currencies feel it (Chapter 25).

Terms of trade · условия на търговияThe price of a country's exports relative to the price of its imports.

●●16.6 Think at the margin and in consequences

Three habits set economic thinking apart from everyday thinking.

  1. Think at the margin. The question is rarely "all or nothing". The question is "one more or not". The first trade of the day on an A+ setup has one expectancy. The fifth, once there are no more A+ setups, has a completely different one. The decision is about the next one, not the average.
  2. Ask "and then what?" Sowell calls this thinking beyond stage one. Rent control looks good in the first month. Five years later there are no new buildings. Low rates look good for loans. Two years later there may be inflation.
  3. Separate intention from result. The law says what is wanted. The incentives say what will happen.

Debrief

What you take from Dossier 16

  • Scarcity means choice; every choice is a trade-off. Central banks choose too.
  • Opportunity cost: the cost of cash is the missed interest, the cost of gold is the missed yield.
  • Judge by incentives, not intentions. Capital flows to where it is rewarded.
  • Ceiling → shortage; floor → surplus. A fixed rate is a price control on a currency.
  • Trade is positive-sum; what a country exports moves its currency.
  • Think about the next unit and ask "and then what?".

A made-up country with a fixed rate of 10 local units per 1 dollar. Inflation there is 40% a year, in the US 3%. The government refuses to change the rate and bans selling dollars outside the banks. On the street the dollar trades at 16.

Task: explain what is happening using the concepts from this chapter (ceiling or floor, shortage or surplus, incentives). What is more likely to happen to the official rate over the next 12 months? Who wins and who loses from the current situation? What signals would you watch as an analyst?

Sample answer
  1. The official price of the dollar (10) is a ceiling below the market price (about 16). Result: a shortage of dollars in the banks and a black market.
  2. Incentives: exporters hide dollars abroad, importers pay bribes for access to the official rate, citizens buy dollars on the street and goods "just in case".
  3. The inflation gap (40% versus 3%) makes the local currency more overvalued every month. The central bank's reserves melt away. It is more likely that the rate gets devalued or floated within the next year than that it holds. This is a probability, not a certainty; IMF help or large exports can buy time.
  4. Winners: those with access to the official rate. Losers: exporters, savers in local currency, people without connections.
  5. Signals: FX reserves, the gap between the official and the street rate, IMF talks, import restrictions, statements that "there will be no devaluation".
