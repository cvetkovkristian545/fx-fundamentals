How the Dollar System Is Built

The dollar is our currency, but your problem. John Connally, US Treasury Secretary, to his European counterparts, 1971

After this chapter you will be able to

  • tell the story of the changing world currencies: Spain, the Netherlands, Britain, the US;
  • explain Bretton Woods, 1971 and the petrodollar in a few sentences;
  • say why the dollar is the reserve currency and what would have to happen for it to stop being one;
  • tell de-dollarisation as a slow signal apart from headline noise.

●24.1 Empires and their money

Since the 15th century every leading power has also had the leading currency. The Spanish silver real was the first global coin. Then the Amsterdam banks made the guilder the currency of trade. After Napoleon, the British pound and London financed the world until the First World War. After the Second World War the baton passed to the dollar.

Handovers rarely come overnight. The pound lost first place over decades, not in a year. Two things repeat at every handover: a big war and a big debt. The world wars were resets of power: after them the new rules of money were written.

Mentor's note

The history of reserve currencies is slow. The headline market is fast. If someone tells you the dollar will lose its status "this year", ask how many years it took the pound. The answer is about thirty.

●24.2 From Bretton Woods to the petrodollar

July 1944, Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. 44 countries agree on post-war money. The dollar is tied to gold at 35 dollars an ounce. The other currencies are tied to the dollar at nearly fixed rates. The IMF and the World Bank are created. The US holds most of the world's gold and is the only large creditor. The dollar becomes the centre.

15 August 1971. In the 1960s the US spends on the Vietnam War and social programmes, and the dollars held abroad come to exceed the gold in Fort Knox. France and others start asking for gold in exchange for their dollars. President Nixon "temporarily" suspends convertibility. The temporary measure lasts to this day. By 1973 the major currencies start floating freely. Since then the world has lived with fiat money (Chapter 17).

The problem was described as early as 1960 by the economist Robert Triffin: if a national currency is the world's reserve, the country must keep sending more and more of it abroad, and sooner or later that undermines trust in it. This is called the Triffin dilemma.

1974. After the oil embargo the US and Saudi Arabia reach an agreement: oil is sold in dollars, and Saudi revenues are invested in US bonds in exchange for security and weapons. Other producers follow. So the world needs dollars to buy energy, and the dollars return to the US as loans. This round trip is called petrodollar recycling.

September 1985, the Plaza Hotel, New York. The dollar is so strong that US exporters are suffering. The five largest economies agree to bring it down together. Over two years the dollar falls sharply against the mark and the yen. The lesson: even the dollar gets managed when the powerful agree.

1 January 1999. The euro appears as the second major reserve currency. Today it is about a fifth of world reserves. Its strength is the size of the euro area; its weakness is that it has no common fiscal policy and no common debt on the scale of the US (Chapter 27: the 2010–2012 crisis).

2008. The financial crisis comes from the US, but the world runs to the dollar, not away from it. Banks around the world need dollars to pay their dollar debts. The Fed opens swap lines with other central banks and effectively becomes the lender of last resort for the whole world. It shows how deeply the dollar is built in.

●●24.3 Why the dollar

Depth

The US bond market is the largest and most liquid in the world. You can buy or sell billions in minutes.

Law

Predictable courts and protection of property. A foreigner trusts they will get their money back.

Network

Almost everything is priced in dollars: oil, metals, loans. Everyone uses dollars because everyone else uses them.

Power

The largest military guards the sea lanes along which trade moves.

French minister Valéry Giscard d'Estaing called this an "exorbitant privilege": the US can borrow in its own currency, and the world wants it. That is why the dollar behaves as a safe haven even when the US runs twin deficits.

StudentThen why does the dollar sometimes strengthen when the crisis comes from the US itself?

MentorBecause the world owes trillions in dollars. When things get scary, everyone has to find dollars to pay. Some call this the "dollar milkshake": the dollar sucks liquidity out of the world. It is not fair, but it is mechanics.

Exorbitant privilegeThe outsized privilege of borrowing in your own currency.
Eurodollar · евродоларDollars deposited and lent outside the US.
COFERThe IMF's statistics on the composition of FX reserves.

●●●24.4 Debt and financial repression

A country with a large debt has four ways out: grow faster than the interest rate, run budget surpluses, default, or keep rates below inflation for years so the debt melts away in real terms. The last is called financial repression. It is not a conspiracy theory but documented policy: economists Carmen Reinhart and Belen Sbrancia describe how after the Second World War the US and Britain melted their debts in exactly this way.

An example from history: the US came out of the war with debt of about 106% of GDP (1946). Rates were held low, and inflation was at times high. By the mid-1970s the debt had fallen to about a quarter of GDP. Not because it was repaid, but because the economy in dollars grew faster than it.

Some authors, among them Andrei Jikh and Luke Gromen, whose ideas appear in the notes, argue that the US today is returning to a similar policy by more hidden routes: more short-term bonds, rules that make banks and stablecoin issuers hold government debt, pressure on the central bank. The concept itself is recognised; how much of it is a plan and how much a reaction is a matter of interpretation. For a currency trader the takeaway is simple: real rates below zero for a long time are bad for the currency in the long run and good for gold, even when in the short run the market trades a hawkish central bank.

Financial repression · финансова репресияKeeping rates below inflation to melt the debt away.
Primary deficit · първичен дефицитThe deficit excluding interest payments.
Fiscal dominance · фискално господствоThe debt is so large that the central bank cannot raise rates freely.

●●24.5 De-dollarisation: signal or noise

De-dollarisation is the idea that the world is slowly reducing its dependence on the dollar. There are real signs: the sanctions of 2014 and 2022 showed that dollar reserves can be frozen; central banks are buying gold at a record pace; part of the trade between China, Russia and others is settled in yuan and roubles. There is also a lot of noise: every statement at a BRICS summit becomes the headline "the end of the dollar".

Signal (slow, measurable)Noise (fast, headline)
The dollar's share of reserves (IMF COFER, quarterly)"BRICS announced a common currency"
Central bank gold purchases (World Gold Council)"Country X stopped using dollars" with no numbers
Foreign holdings of US bonds (TIC data)A single oil deal in yuan
Currency shares in SWIFT payments"The dollar will collapse by the end of the year"

The notes this book grew out of also describe two competing futures that Andrei Jikh talks about: a dollar that renews itself through stablecoins backed by US bonds ("the petrodollar rebuilt for the internet"), versus a world in which part of trade is settled through gold. This is a scenario to think about, not a forecast. It is useful because it tells you what to watch: stablecoin regulation, gold purchases and bond holdings.

Warning

De-dollarisation is a horizon of years to decades. It is not a reason to short USD today at 10:15. If your H1 setup is bullish for the dollar, an end-of-the-dollar thesis does not make it wrong. The two horizons do not mix (Chapter 28).

Debrief

What you take from Dossier 24

  • The leading power has the leading currency; handovers take decades and pass through wars and debt.
  • 1944: the dollar to gold. 1971: the end of gold. 1974: the petrodollar. 2008: the Fed as the world's lender.
  • The dollar is the reserve because of depth, law, network and power.
  • Financial repression is a real tool: rates below inflation melt the debt and weaken the currency in the long run.
  • De-dollarisation is measured with COFER, gold and TIC, not with headlines.

Sunday evening. Headline: "A group of countries announces it will settle part of its trade in gold". On X people are talking about "the end of the dollar". On Friday DXY closed at a weekly high, the US 2y yield is rising, and US CPI is on Tuesday. Gold has been in a strong uptrend for months, and central banks are buying according to the latest quarterly data.

Task: how do you separate the signal from the noise? Which horizons are mixed up here? What would change your view of the dollar this week, and what over the coming years? How would you use the headline, if at all?

Sample answer
  1. The headline is an announcement without numbers: how much trade, when, by what mechanism. Until I see volumes, for this week it is noise.
  2. The horizons: the week is driven by CPI and yields (bullish for USD now); the years by reserves and gold (slowly bearish for USD). They do not mix.
  3. This week: CPI versus forecast, the 2y yield, the Fed's tone. The years: the next COFER, gold purchases, TIC data.
  4. The headline supports the long-term thesis for gold, which is already visible in central bank buying. For intraday: it may give a short-term spike on Monday in Asia, but the protocol and the structure decide. It is not a reason to short USD before CPI.
