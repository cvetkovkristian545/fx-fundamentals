Money, Banks, Inflation

Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon. Milton Friedman

After this chapter you will be able to

  • trace the path from barter to fiat and digital money;
  • show how banks create money when they lend;
  • tell CPI, core CPI, PCE and PPI apart and why the market watches exactly these;
  • explain why inflation data move a currency.

●17.1 From barter to gold to fiat

Without money, trade is barter: the shoemaker who wants bread has to find a baker who needs shoes, today. Money solves this. It has three jobs: medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value.

AntiquityCommodity moneySalt, cattle, shells, then metal coins. The value is in the object itself.
17th–19th c.Goldsmiths' receiptsGoldsmiths keep other people's gold and issue receipts that start being used as money. That is how banking is born.
19th c. – 1971The gold standard and Bretton WoodsPaper money can be exchanged for gold. Gold does not "give value"; it limits the amount of money (Chapter 24).
1971Fiat moneyThe US stops exchanging dollars for gold. Since then money is accepted by law and by trust, with nothing behind it but the institutions.
TodayDigital moneyMost money is entries in bank accounts. Add stablecoins, cryptocurrencies and central bank digital currencies (CBDC, Chapter 40).
Mentor's note

A fiat currency is only as strong as the institutions behind it. When trust in a central bank falls, people flee to gold, dollars or crypto. That is why gold sometimes rises together with the dollar: that is fear, not rate logic.

Barter · бартерExchanging goods for goods without money.
Fiat money · фиатни париMoney with no intrinsic value, accepted by law and trust.
Store of value · съхранение на стойностSomething that keeps its purchasing power over time.

●●17.2 How banks create money

A bank does not keep all deposits in the vault. It holds a reserve and lends out the rest. The borrower pays someone, who deposits it in their own bank, which also lends out part of it. That way one deposit turns into several. This is fractional reserve banking.

Textbooks describe this with a multiplier: with a 10% reserve the theoretical maximum is 10 times the original deposit. In reality the number is smaller, because it depends on how many people want loans and how much they hold in cash. Central banks today also describe it the other way round: the loan creates the deposit, not the deposit the loan.

Myth

A bank only lends the money people have deposited.

Reality

When a bank makes a loan, it creates a new deposit in the borrower's account. What limits it are capital rules, loan demand and the central bank's rate, not the exact amount of deposits.

This system works until people want their money back all at once. Then comes a bank run: the bank may be solvent, but its assets (mortgages, buildings) are not liquid. Between 1930 and 1933 more than 9,000 US banks failed. Today deposits are guaranteed up to a threshold (in the EU €100,000), and the central bank is the lender of last resort.

Fractional reserve · частичен резервThe bank keeps only part of its deposits as a reserve.
Bank run · банкова паникаMany depositors withdraw at once.
Liquidity · ликвидностHow fast something becomes cash without losing value.
Lender of last resortThe central bank that lends to banks in a crisis.

●17.3 What inflation is

Inflation is a general rise in prices. Not one thing getting more expensive, but almost everything. The flip side: every euro buys less. At 3% inflation a year prices double in about 23 years. At 10%, in about 7.

Where does inflation come from? Economists split the causes into two groups:

Demand-pull inflationCost-push inflation
What it is calledDemand-pullCost-push
CauseToo much money and credit relative to goodsA jump in costs: energy, raw materials, wages
GrowthStrong, above capacityWeak or falling
Example2021: stimulus and low rates after COVID1973: the oil embargo
Difficulty for the central bankEasier: it raises ratesHarder: hiking hits growth that is already weak

When both mix with high unemployment, you get stagflation: prices rise while the economy stands still. It is the hardest regime for a central bank and for most assets (Chapter 22).

Inflation · инфлацияA general rise in prices; money loses purchasing power.
Deflation · дефлацияA general fall in prices. Dangerous because debts become heavier.
Stagflation · стагфлацияHigh inflation plus weak growth and high unemployment.

●●17.4 How it is measured: CPI, core, PCE, PPI

Every month government statisticians "shop" for the same basket of goods and services and calculate how much more it costs. There are several measures and the market watches them all, but with different weight.

MeasureWhat it measuresWhy it mattersWhen it comes out (US)
CPIConsumer prices, the whole basketThe most-watched number; moves everything with USDAround mid-month, 15:30 Sofia
Core CPICPI excluding food and energyLess noise; shows the trend. The market often reacts more to itTogether with CPI
PCE / Core PCEA broader measure of household spendingThe Fed's 2% target is on PCE. Usually 0.2–0.5 pp below CPITowards the end of the month
PPIThe prices producers receiveOften leads CPI: pressure shows up at producers firstAround CPI, often a day before or after
HICPThe euro area's harmonised indexThe ECB's 2% target; has an early (flash) estimate at the end of the monthEurostat, 12:00 Sofia

Each measure has two versions: m/m (versus last month) and y/y (versus the same month last year). Traders watch both. A y/y number can fall just because last year's month was very high (base effect), while m/m shows the pressure is still there.

Watch on Forex Factory

On CPI day the calendar has several rows at the same time: "CPI m/m", "Core CPI m/m", "CPI y/y". Usually all three have a red folder, and the market most often watches Core CPI m/m. If the two point in different directions (hot headline, cold core), the first move often reverses. Chapter 19 shows how to read them.

CPI · индекс на потребителските цениThe price of a basket of consumer goods and services.
Core · базиснаExcluding food and energy.
m/m, y/yMonth on month, year on year.
Base effect · базов ефектA change in y/y that comes from last year's base.

●●17.5 Why inflation moves the currency

The chain is short and you should know it by heart:

  1. Inflation comes in higher than expected.
  2. The market expects the central bank to raise rates or keep them high for longer.
  3. The higher rate makes the currency pay more than the others (opportunity cost, Chapter 16).
  4. Capital flows to it. The currency strengthens, while gold and stocks often get cheaper.
Fed expectations

More hawkish: fewer or later rate cuts.

USD

Stronger. EURUSD down, USDJPY up.

Gold

Usually down: real yields rise.

Stocks

Under pressure: more expensive money.

Fed expectations

More dovish: earlier rate cuts.

USD

Weaker. EURUSD up, USDJPY down.

Gold

Usually up.

Stocks

Usually up.

These are typical reactions, not laws. If the central bank has already signalled it will "look through" a temporary spike (for example from energy), the reaction is weaker. If inflation is so high that people lose trust in the central bank, the currency can fall despite high rates. That happens in countries like Turkey and Argentina.

StudentSo higher inflation is good for the currency?

MentorFor a week, if the bank is credible. For ten years, never. In the short run the market trades rates. In the long run a currency with higher inflation loses purchasing power and weakens. Chapter 20 brings the two horizons together.

Hawkish · ястребовLeaning towards higher rates.
Dovish · гълъбовLeaning towards lower rates.
Real yield · реална доходностThe nominal yield minus expected inflation.

●●●17.6 MV = PQ and hyperinflation

The quantity theory of money says: money × velocity = prices × output. If money grows faster than goods and people spend at the same speed, prices rise. If people lose trust and start spending money as soon as they get it, velocity (V) jumps and prices rise even faster than money.

CaseWhat happensHow it ends
Germany 1920–1923From about 40 marks per dollar to over 4 trillionA new currency (the Rentenmark), a hard cap on issuance
Zimbabwe 2007–2008An attempt to cut prices by decree; shops empty outSwitching to foreign currencies (dollar, rand)
Bulgaria 1996–1997A banking crisis, then hyperinflation; in early 1997 monthly inflation exceeds 200% and the dollar briefly passes about 3,000 levaA currency board from July 1997: the lev pegged to the mark, later to the euro (Chapter 18)
Warning

Hyperinflation does not start with one bad year. It starts when a government finances its deficit with new money and people stop believing it will stop. The signals: a black market in currency, melting reserves, "temporary" limits on withdrawals.

Velocity · скорост на паритеHow many times a unit of money changes hands in a year.
Hyperinflation · хиперинфлацияPrice rises above 50% a month, by convention.
Currency board · валутен бордA regime in which local money is fully backed by a reserve currency at a fixed rate.
Debrief

What you take from Dossier 17

  • Money is a medium of exchange, a unit of account and a store of value. Fiat money rests on trust.
  • Banks create money when they lend. The system is vulnerable to panic.
  • Inflation comes from demand or from costs; stagflation is the hardest combination.
  • CPI, Core CPI, PCE, PPI, HICP: know which one is which central bank's target.
  • Hotter inflation than expected → higher rates → a stronger currency, in the short run and with a credible bank.
  • Hyperinflation is a crisis of trust; Bulgaria 1997 is a lesson close to home.
