Geopolitics and World Order

We have no eternal allies, and we have no perpetual enemies. Our interests are eternal and perpetual. Lord Palmerston, 1848

After this chapter you will be able to

  • describe the interests and levers of the US, China, the EU, Russia and the Middle East;
  • explain how tariffs and trade wars reach currencies;
  • say why narrow sea passages matter for oil and for CAD, NOK and JPY;
  • turn a headline into scenarios with probabilities and tell a testable forecast from an untestable one.

●25.1 The players: interests and levers

Geopolitics is economics with armies. Every great power has interests (what it wants to keep or achieve) and levers (what it can use to press others). When you see a headline, the first question is: which player does this serve, and with what lever?

Mentor's note

A trader does not need to know who will win a war. They need to know which asset reacts to which risk and how much of that risk is already in the price. Those two things are much more modest and much more useful.

●●25.2 Tariffs and trade wars

A tariff is a tax on imports. Politically it sounds like protecting "our" jobs. Economically the effect is usually different: Sowell points out that US steel tariffs save thousands of jobs in steelmaking but cost more jobs in the industries that buy steel. The best-known example is the Smoot-Hawley Act of 1930: the highest US tariffs in a century, retaliatory tariffs from the whole world, and by 1933 world trade had shrunk to about a third.

How tariffs reach currencies:

  1. Inflation in the country imposing the tariff. Imports get dearer. The central bank may hold off on cuts, which sometimes supports the currency.
  2. A hit to the target country's growth. Its currency usually weakens (for example the yuan under US tariffs).
  3. Risk-off. If the escalation is big, the market sells risk: stocks and commodity currencies down, safe havens up.
  4. Retaliation and negotiations. The market trades every headline about escalation or a pause. Volatility stays high as long as there is uncertainty.

In April 2025 the US announced broad "reciprocal" tariffs. Markets had one of their worst stretches in years, and a week later much of the tariffs was postponed by 90 days. The lesson for the trader: in a trade war a headline can reverse price within minutes, which is why the high-volatility sizing rule (Chapter 11) matters more than the forecast.

Tariff · митоA tax on imported goods.
Retaliation · ответни меркиTariffs in response to tariffs.
Protectionism · протекционизъмProtecting local producers against imports.

●●25.3 Energy and the chokepoints

Much of world trade passes through a few narrow sea passages. If one of them is closed, the prices of oil, gas and freight jump, and the currencies of energy importers and exporters react in opposite directions.

What passes through

About a fifth of the world's oil and much of the liquefied gas from the Persian Gulf.

Who loses if it closes

Energy importers: Japan, Korea, India, Europe, China. JPY and EUR under pressure.

Who gains

Exporters outside the Gulf: Canada, Norway, the US. CAD and NOK supported.

Other

Gold and the dollar as safe havens; inflation expectations up.

What passes through

The route between Asia and Europe through the Red Sea and the Suez Canal.

If it closes

Ships go around Africa: weeks longer, dearer transport, slower deliveries.

Market

Goods inflation in Europe, pressure on EUR, higher freight rates.

Example

Houthi attacks on ships from late 2023 diverted much of the traffic.

What passes through

Most of the oil and goods for China, Japan and Korea from the Middle East and Africa.

Risk

A hit to Chinese supplies; AUD and NZD via Chinese demand.

Probability

A closure is unlikely, but it is common in Taiwan conflict scenarios.

Market

Oil up, CNH and Asian currencies down.

What passes through

Trade between Asia and the US East Coast.

Risk

Drought and low water levels limited transits (2023).

Politics

US claims to control have been in the news since 2025.

Market

Freight and grain prices; a weaker effect on the major pairs.

According to the notes this book grew out of, 2026 gives a live example: after strikes on Iran in late February the Strait of Hormuz was closed, oil rose sharply in March, and later the approaches to the Red Sea came under threat too. The consequences run through the whole chain from Chapter 22: oil → inflation expectations → yields → the dollar → gold. The specific numbers come from the notes and have not been checked separately; the mechanics are what you learn.

●●25.4 Technology and AI as a market force

The rivalry between the US and China today is also technological: chips, rare metals, artificial intelligence. Notes from the Bulgarian channel "Кибер Хора" highlight a few ideas worth keeping in mind as an analyst:

  • AI takes over tasks, not whole professions, at least at first. The effect on employment is gradual but can speed up.
  • The gains go first to the owners of capital: the companies and states that own the computing power.
  • The bubble and the infrastructure: even if AI company valuations collapse, the infrastructure built stays, like the railways and fibre-optic cables after their bubbles.
  • For Bulgaria: the country is among the last in the EU in AI use, but it is exposed through the thousands of outsourcing jobs.

For currencies this is a slow driver: productivity, growth differences between the US and Europe, capital flows to tech markets. For the day it matters more when it becomes stock market news: a big drop in tech stocks is a risk-off day for everyone.

Productivity · производителностOutput per hour of work. The long-term driver of wealth.

●●●25.5 From headline to probability

Geopolitics is the hardest part for a trader because it is noisy and emotional. So here we work strictly. A headline is not a signal. A headline is a reason to build scenarios, give them probabilities and see what is already in the price.

  1. What is the fact? Separate the event from the opinion and the forecast in the headline.
  2. Base rate. How often have similar events historically led to a major escalation? Usually less often than the headlines suggest.
  3. Three scenarios. De-escalation, status quo, escalation. Give them percentages that add up to 100.
  4. Assets. For each scenario: oil, gold, USD, JPY, EUR, indices.
  5. The price. How much of the escalation is already priced? If oil has jumped 10% in a day, part of the bad scenario has already been paid for.
  6. What would change your mind? A specific event that would make you reassess.
Base rate · базова честотаHow often something has happened historically.
Scenario · сценарийA possible outcome with a probability.

●●25.6 Testable or untestable forecast

The internet is full of people who "predicted" everything. Before you give a forecast weight, check five things: what exactly will happen, by when, how you will measure it, what would refute it and whether the author records both their hits and their misses. A forecast with no deadline and no way to be refuted cannot be wrong. So it cannot be useful either.

Debrief

What you take from Dossier 25

  • Every player has interests and levers; ask whom the news serves.
  • Tariffs raise inflation at home, hit the target's growth and can trigger risk-off.
  • Chokepoints connect geopolitics with oil and with CAD, NOK, JPY, EUR.
  • AI is a slow driver of productivity and a fast driver of the stock market.
  • Headline → scenarios → probabilities → what is in the price → what would change your mind.
  • A forecast with no deadline and no measure cannot be wrong, so it is not useful.

Monday, 08:00 Sofia. Over the weekend: an attack on a tanker near Hormuz. Brent opens with a +7% gap. Gold +1.2%. USDJPY −0.4%. EURUSD opens 25 pips lower. FOMC is on Wednesday. Your D1 bias for EURUSD has been bullish since Friday.

Task: build three scenarios for the week with probabilities. Which assets does each scenario move? How much of the escalation looks already priced? What would change your mind? What happens to your bias and your size?

Sample answer
  1. Fact: one attack, one tanker. Opinion: "the start of a war" in the headlines.
  2. Scenarios (example): de-escalation 35% (the incident stays isolated, the gap fills), status quo with tension 45% (oil stays high, volatility), escalation 20% (more attacks, a closure).
  3. Assets: escalation lifts oil, gold, USD, CAD, NOK; it hits EUR and JPY via energy, but JPY can gain as a safe haven. De-escalation reverses the gaps.
  4. In the price: +7% overnight is a significant part of the "tension" scenario. Escalation is not fully priced.
  5. Trigger: a second attack or an official closure → the escalation probability goes up; a diplomatic channel → down.
  6. Bias: Friday's bullish bias is in question; the verdict is YELLOW until the H4 structure is checked. Size: half or skip until FOMC, because volatility is high and the week has two big events.
