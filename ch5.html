Thinking in Probabilities

It's not whether you're right or wrong that's important, but how much money you make when you're right and how much you lose when you're wrong. George Soros (as told by Stanley Druckenmiller)

After this chapter you will be able to

  • explain why someone who wins only 4 trades out of 10 can be profitable;
  • calculate the expected value of an approach;
  • write three scenarios for tomorrow with approximate probabilities and invalidation conditions.

●5.1 There is no right and wrong, only more likely and less likely

You roll a die. You say “it won't be a six”. A six comes up. Were you wrong? No. Your decision was good: the odds were 5 to 1 in your favour. The outcome was bad. These are two different things, and if you mix them up you will learn the wrong lessons.

The market is the same, except you don't know how many sides the die has. No analysis is “right”. It is an estimate of which scenario is more likely. A good analyst is not the one who guesses correctly. A good analyst is the one who judges the odds more accurately than average and loses little when wrong.

Good decision · good outcome

A deserved win. Repeat the process.

Good decision · bad outcome

A normal loss. Change nothing.

Bad decision · good outcome

Dangerous luck. It teaches you bad habits.

Bad decision · bad outcome

A deserved loss. Fix the process.

Mentor's note

The most expensive trade you will ever make is a winning trade without a plan. It teaches you that a plan isn't needed, and the bill comes later.

Probability · вероятностHow often you expect something to happen over many repetitions.
Process vs outcome · процес и резултатThe quality of a decision and what happened are different things.

●5.2 Expected value and R

In trading we measure results not in money but in R. 1R is the amount you risk on one trade (the distance to the stop). If you risk 50 dollars and win 150, that is +3R. If the stop is hit, that is −1R. This lets you compare trades across different accounts and sizes.

Expected value (expectancy) tells you how many R you make on average per trade if you repeat the approach many times:

E = (% winners × average win in R) − (% losers × average loss in R)

Example: you win 40% of trades with an average of +3R and lose 60% at −1R. E = 0.40 × 3 − 0.60 × 1 = 1.2 − 0.6 = +0.6R per trade. You lose most trades and are still profitable. That is why in this book the target of every planned trade is at least 3R: it lets you be wrong often.

StudentThen why not just aim for 10R on every trade?

MentorBecause the further the target, the less often price reaches it. The win rate falls. The art is in the balance, not in the biggest number. 3R is a sensible middle for the approach in this book.

R · риск единицаThe amount you lose if the stop is hit. Profit is measured in multiples of R.
Expectancy · очаквана стойностThe average result per trade in R over many repetitions.
Win rate · процент печелившиThe share of trades that end in profit.

●5.3 Losing streaks are normal

Even with a good approach there will be long losing streaks. At a 55% win rate over 50 trades, seeing 4 to 6 losses in a row somewhere along the way is normal. At 40% a streak of 6 to 10 losses is no surprise. The simulation above shows them: look for the longest red streak.

That is why the rule in Chapter 36 is: no conclusions before at least 20 trades and no changes to the approach because of a short losing streak. If you change the rules after every streak of three losses, you will never know whether the approach works.

Myth

Five losses in a row mean the strategy is broken.

Reality

Five losses in a row at a 40–55% win rate are statistically normal. A broken strategy shows itself over 30–50 trades, not five.

Losing streak · серия загубиA run of losing trades one after another.
Sample size · размер на извадкатаHow many trades you need before you can say something about the approach.
Drawdown · спадHow far the account has fallen from its last peak.

●●5.4 The scenario tree: A, B, C

Instead of asking “where will price go?”, ask: “what are the possible paths, how likely is each and what would tell me I'm wrong?”. That is the scenario tree. Every analysis in this book ends with one.

Quality rule: every scenario needs a testable invalidation condition. “If the market turns bearish” is not testable. “If H1 closes below 1.0815” is.

Scenario · сценарийOne possible price path with the conditions under which it holds.
Invalidation · обезсилванеThe specific event that shows the scenario is wrong.
Base rate · базова честотаHow often something happens in general, before you look at the specific case.

●●5.5 How to update a view

Scenarios are not promises. When new information arrives (price reaches a level, news comes out), the probabilities shift. A good analyst shifts them calmly and in proportion. A bad one does one of two things: doesn't shift them at all or flips them completely after one candle.

The four most common mental traps:

TrapWhat it looks likeAntidote
Confirmation biasYou look only for the news and signals that say you are right.Before the analysis, write down what would make you change your mind.
AnchoringYou stick to the first price or the first opinion you heard.Start every day from D1 and afresh, as if you didn't know yesterday's analysis.
RecencyYour last three trades look like the truth about the approach.Look at at least 20–30 trades in the journal, not the last three.
NarrativeA good story (about wars, about the end of the dollar) replaces the data.Ask: what exactly do I need to see in price for the story to be true? (Chapter 25)

News is the most common reason to update your scenarios. Before every red folder write down: if the number is higher than expected, which scenario wins? If it is lower? That way your reaction is decided before the emotion arrives.

No single right answer

You have 12 trades in your journal: 4 winners (+3R, +3R, +2.5R, +3R) and 8 losers (−1R each). The last five are losses in a row. You want to change the approach and switch to 1R targets “to win more often”.

Task: calculate the result so far in R. Is there enough data for a conclusion? What would happen to the expected value if you switched to 1R but the win rate rose only to 50%?

Sample answer
  1. Wins: 3 + 3 + 2.5 + 3 = 11.5R. Losses: 8 × 1 = 8R. Result: +3.5R over 12 trades, about +0.29R per trade. The approach is profitable despite the five losses.
  2. 12 trades are too few for a conclusion. The rule is at least 20, and 30–50 for a real assessment.
  3. With a 1R target and 50% winners: E = 0.5 × 1 − 0.5 × 1 = 0. Zero, before costs. After spread and commissions it is negative.
  4. Conclusion: the five losses are an emotional signal, not a statistical one. I stick to the plan, log every trade and review at 20.
