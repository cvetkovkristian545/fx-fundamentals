Crises and Debt Cycles

Stability is destabilising. Hyman Minsky

After this chapter you will be able to

  • explain how a bubble is born, grows and bursts using Minsky's model;
  • recognise the common features of eight major crises from 1929 to 2023;
  • explain why a fixed exchange rate falls "all at once" after looking healthy for a long time;
  • judge a country's debt with three numbers and recognise when a currency and its bonds fall together.

●27.1 Why crises repeat

Crises look different: once it is housing, once banks, once government debt, once a virus. Under the surface there is almost always the same thing: credit. People and states borrow against the future while it is cheap and calm. The longer it stays calm, the more they borrow. One day something small makes them want their money back at the same time, and the cash is not there.

The economist Hyman Minsky described this in five steps. Click each point on the curve.

Myth

"This time is different."

Reality

The technology, the asset and the heroes change. The mechanics of credit and the crowd stay. When you hear this phrase from many people at once, you are already in phase 3.

Leverage · ливъриджWorking with borrowed money. It multiplies both the gain and the loss.
Minsky moment · моментът на МинскиThe point at which debts can no longer be serviced from income and forced selling begins.
Deleveraging · сваляне на дългMass repayment of loans and selling of assets to reduce leverage.

●●27.2 Eight crises, one logic

You do not need to remember dates. You need to see the pattern: what inflates, what pricks it, what the currency does in the first days and what was visible beforehand.

What inflates
Stocks bought with borrowed money (on margin), after a decade of optimism.
The pin
The stock market crash of October 1929. The Dow loses about 89% from peak to trough in 1932.
What deepens it
Thousands of bank failures, the money in circulation shrinks by about a third, the Fed tightens in 1931, the Smoot-Hawley tariffs (1930) shrink world trade to about a third.
Currencies
The gold standard breaks up: Britain leaves it in 1931, the US devalues the dollar against gold in 1933. The countries that leave earlier recover earlier.
Lesson
Deflation plus debt is a deadly combination. That is why today's central banks react quickly and massively.
What inflates
Inflation expectations, after years of loose policy and the end of the dollar–gold link (1971).
The pin
The oil shocks of 1973 and 1979.
What deepens it
Price controls (they create shortages), a Fed that raises rates too little and too late.
Currencies
The dollar weakens for most of the decade; gold rises from 35 to about 850 dollars an ounce (January 1980). The turn comes with Paul Volcker, who takes rates to about 20% in 1981, and so begins the strong dollar of the first half of the 1980s.
Lesson
Once unanchored, expectations come back only with pain. That is why central banks today talk so much about "anchored expectations".
What inflates
The British pound, tied in the ERM to the German mark at too high a level.
The pin
Germany keeps rates high after reunification; Britain is in recession and cannot follow for long.
What happens
On 16 September 1992 ("Black Wednesday") the Bank of England raises the rate from 10 to 12% and promises 15%. The market does not believe it. By evening the pound has left the ERM. George Soros makes about a billion dollars.
Lesson
Defending an exchange rate with rates only works if the economy can bear the rates. The market tests exactly that.
What inflates
Short-term dollar debt of companies and banks in Thailand, Indonesia, Korea; local currencies tied to the dollar.
The pin
Thailand floats the baht on 2 July 1997. It loses about half its value within months; the Indonesian rupiah loses about 80%.
What deepens it
Currency mismatch: revenues in local currency, debts in dollars. Every fall in the currency increases the debt. Indonesian GDP falls about 13% in 1998.
Currencies
The contagion spreads: Russia 1998, then the LTCM fund. Afterwards the Asian countries build huge FX reserves so it does not happen again.
Lesson
Current account deficit + short-term foreign-currency debt + fixed rate = a recipe for crisis.
What inflates
US housing financed with mortgages to people who cannot pay them, sliced up and resold around the world.
The pin
House prices stop rising. Bear Stearns (March 2008), then Lehman Brothers fails on 15 September.
Currencies
Paradox: the crisis is American, but the dollar strengthens, because the whole world owes dollars and rushes to find them. The yen strengthens even more as carry positions close: AUDJPY falls almost by half between summer and the end of October 2008.
Response
Rates near zero, QE, Fed swap lines to other central banks.
Lesson
In a panic the world buys the currency it owes. That is the dollar.
What inflates
Government debt and private bubbles on the euro area's periphery: Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain, Italy.
The pin
Greece admits its deficit is much bigger than reported (late 2009).
What deepens it
The "doom loop": banks hold their own state's bonds, the state bails out the banks. One common rate but no common budget. The Greek 10y yield passes 30% in 2012.
Currencies
EURUSD falls from about 1.51 (late 2009) to about 1.19 (June 2010). The turn comes with one phrase: Mario Draghi, July 2012, "whatever it takes".
Lesson
Watch the spread between the weak and the strong country's bonds (BTP–Bund). It is the euro's thermometer.
What inflates
Nothing special: this is an external shock. That is why it is instructive.
The pin
The lockdowns of March 2020.
Currencies
The "dash for cash": everything is sold for dollars, even gold for a few days. The S&P loses about 34% in a month. DXY jumps close to 103. In April WTI oil briefly trades below zero.
Response
The biggest stimulus in history: rates at zero, unlimited QE, swap lines, huge budget programmes.
Lesson
In a systemic shock the first reaction is the dollar, then central banks flood the market and the dollar weakens. The two phases can be days apart.
What inflates
Demand fuelled by stimulus versus supply limited by broken chains and then by the war in Ukraine.
The pin
Central banks call inflation "transitory" in 2021 and fall behind.
What happens
US inflation reaches 9.1% (June 2022), euro area 10.6% (October 2022). The Fed hikes at the fastest pace since the 1980s.
Currencies
A strong dollar: EURUSD falls below parity, USDJPY reaches about 152 because the Bank of Japan keeps rates at zero. The pound crashes in the Truss crisis (see 27.4).
Lesson
The currency of the central bank that tightens fastest wins. The currency of the one that stands still loses.

●●27.3 Why a fixed rate falls "all at once"

A central bank defending a fixed rate buys its own currency with its reserves every time someone sells it. From the outside the rate looks motionless. On the inside the reserves are draining. The market is not watching the rate but how many days are left.

This is the flip side of what you saw with Bulgaria in Chapter 18. The 1997 currency board survived because every lev was backed by reserves and the state stopped spending more than it could pay. A peg does not protect by itself. The reserves and the discipline behind it do.

Peg · фиксиран курсA currency tied to another at a set level.
Speculative attack · спекулативна атакаMass selling of a currency whose peg the market thinks will not hold.

●●●27.4 When a currency and its bonds fall together

Usually higher rates make a currency more attractive. There is, however, one dangerous case: the yield rises because investors are fleeing the state's bonds, not because the central bank is tightening. Then bonds and currency fall together. The market is saying "I don't believe you". That is emerging-market behaviour, even when the country is rich.

Background
Britain, September 2022. Inflation about 10%, the Bank of England is hiking, debt is high.
Headline
On 23 September Liz Truss's government announces the so-called mini-budget: about £45 bn of tax cuts with no explanation of how they will be paid for.
What price did
GBPUSD falls to about 1.03 on 26 September, a record low. The 30y gilt yield jumps by about 1.3 percentage points in three days. Pension funds with LDI strategies get margin calls and sell bonds, pushing yields up further.
Why
The market is pricing not the rate but trust in the public finances. A higher yield born of fear does not attract money, it drives it away.
Resolution
The Bank of England promises to buy up to £5 bn of bonds a day. The mini-budget is withdrawn. Truss remains prime minister for only 49 days.
Visible beforehand
High debt, high inflation, a weak currency even before the budget, plus a spread widening with no news from the central bank.
Mentor's note

The October 2026 notes track similar stress in France: French bond (OAT) yields versus German ones, a Moody's rating review on 23 October and a budget vote on 17 November. This is not a crisis forecast. It is exactly the scenario in which the rule "higher yield = stronger currency" can flip for the euro. If OATs rally (yields fall) and EURUSD rises, the world is normal. If yields rise and EURUSD falls, the market is talking about trust.

Gilts · гилтовеBritish government bonds.
LDI · Liability-driven investingA pension fund strategy with leverage in bonds. In a crisis it forces selling at the worst moment.
Term premium · срочна премияThe extra yield investors demand to hold long debt under uncertainty.
Fiscal credibility · фискално довериеThe market's belief that the state will pay its debts without inflation.

●●27.5 The debt spiral and the three numbers

In his videos Andrei Jikh paints an alarming picture: world debt of hundreds of trillions of dollars, US debt around 40 trillion, interest above a trillion dollars a year, and trillions of debt that must be refinanced every year at higher rates. The numbers in the notes are his and are for 2026; I do not check them one by one here. The mechanism, however, is real, and you saw it in Chapter 24: if the rate is above growth, the debt grows by itself.

The Crypto Ninjas channel offers a calmer tool: do not look at the absolute size of the debt but at three numbers. They say more than any headline.

  1. The spread over Germany (or over the safest country in the region): how much more the state pays for a 10y loan. It is the market's opinion, expressed in money.
  2. The direction of debt to GDP: not whether it is high, but whether it is rising or falling. Britain had debt above 200% of GDP after the wars and melted it, because growth was above the rate.
  3. Interest as a share of the budget: how much of every lev collected goes on interest alone. In Bulgaria in 1996 interest ate, by estimates, more than half of budget revenue. That is the spiral.

●27.6 The first 72 hours of a crisis

This section is not about predicting crises. It is about surviving when one catches you with an open position. Whatever the cause, the first days look almost the same.

Hour 0The shockA headline or a gap at the open. The broker's spreads widen many times over. Stops may fill far from the level.
Hours 1–12Flight to safe havensFlight to USD, JPY, CHF. Carry positions close: AUDJPY, NZDJPY, MXNJPY fall fastest. Gold is unpredictable: it can fall if someone is selling everything for dollars.
Day 1–2The central banksCentral banks speak. An emergency meeting is called or swap lines and bond purchases are announced. The market starts to price the help.
Day 2–3The first bounceThe first sharp counter-move. Many people mistake it for the end of the crisis. Sometimes it is. Often it is not.
For the scalper

On such days technical levels lose some of their strength, because the big players act under compulsion, not by the chart. The spread can eat your entire stop. That is why the 1% risk rule counts double: if you cannot estimate the spread and the stop slippage, your position size is too big. A missed day is cheaper than a blown account.

Gap · гапA price jump between two quotes with no trades in between.
Slippage · приплъзванеThe difference between the stop price and the price at which it actually fills.
Debrief

What you take from Dossier 27

  • Under almost every crisis there is credit built up in calm times.
  • Minsky: displacement → boom → euphoria → distress → panic. "This time is different" is phase 3.
  • In a panic the world buys the currency it owes: the dollar. Carry closes, the yen rises.
  • A fixed rate falls when the market works out that the reserves or the economy's patience are running out.
  • Yields and the currency falling together mean a crisis of trust, not tightening.
  • Three numbers for debt: spread, direction of debt, interest in the budget.

No single right answer

A made-up euro area country. The government announces a budget with a large deficit and no financing plan. Over two days its 10y yield rises 0.6 pp while the German one barely moves. EURUSD falls 1.1%, EURCHF 1.4%. The central bank is silent. You usually scalp EURUSD in London.

Task: why does the euro fall even though the yield is rising? What is the most important indicator for the coming days? What could the central bank say to reverse the move? How does your way of working with EURUSD change while this lasts?

Sample answer
  1. The yield rises from fear, not from tightening: the spread over Germany widens. It is a question of trust, so the currency falls together with the bonds. EURCHF falls more because the franc is the regional safe haven.
  2. Most important is this country's spread over Germany. As long as it widens, the pressure on the euro stays. Narrowing is the first sign of calm, often before price turns.
  3. A signal from the central bank that it will protect the "transmission" of monetary policy, that is, buy bonds if spreads widen without justification (like "whatever it takes" or the TPI instrument). Or the government withdraws the budget, as in Britain in 2022.
  4. The macro wind is against the euro and the move is news-driven, not structural. It is sensible to cut size, avoid the hours around statements, use the spread as a filter and not look for buys against the direction just because price is "at a level". The decision whether to trade at all remains mine.
